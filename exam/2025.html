Question 11: Describe the various components of cash flow statement.

8 Marks
Answer

A Cash Flow Statement is a financial statement that shows the inflow and outflow of cash and cash equivalents of a business during a particular accounting period. It is mainly divided into the following components:

1. Cash Flow from Operating Activities

Operating activities are the main revenue-generating activities of the business. They show the cash generated or used in normal day-to-day operations.

  • Cash received from customers
  • Cash paid to suppliers
  • Payment of wages and salaries
  • Payment of operating expenses
  • Payment or receipt of taxes and interest, as applicable

A positive operating cash flow indicates that regular business operations are generating sufficient cash.

2. Cash Flow from Investing Activities

Investing activities relate to the purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments.

Cash inflows include:

  • Sale of fixed assets such as land, building and machinery
  • Sale of investments
  • Collection of loans and advances given to others

Cash outflows include:

  • Purchase of fixed assets
  • Purchase of investments
  • Loans and advances given to other parties

3. Cash Flow from Financing Activities

Financing activities are related to changes in the capital structure and long-term borrowings of the business.

Cash inflows include:

  • Issue of shares
  • Issue of debentures or bonds
  • Borrowing of long-term loans

Cash outflows include:

  • Repayment of loans
  • Redemption of debentures
  • Repurchase of shares
  • Payment of dividends

4. Net Increase or Decrease in Cash and Cash Equivalents

The net cash flows from operating, investing and financing activities are combined to determine the overall increase or decrease in cash during the accounting period.

5. Opening and Closing Cash Balance

The statement also shows the opening balance of cash and cash equivalents. After adding or deducting the net change in cash, the closing balance of cash and cash equivalents is obtained.

Conclusion: Thus, the cash flow statement classifies cash transactions into operating, investing and financing activities and helps users understand the liquidity and cash position of the business.

Question 12: Write short notes on (Any TWO): (a) Cash Flow Statement (b) Stock Split and Stock Dividend (c) Business Risk and Financial Risk.

8 Marks — Any Two
Answer

(a) Cash Flow Statement

A Cash Flow Statement is a financial statement that shows the inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents during a particular accounting period. It helps to know the liquidity and cash position of a business.

Cash flows are classified into three activities:

  1. Operating Activities: Cash arising from normal business operations, such as cash received from customers and cash paid to suppliers, employees and for operating expenses.
  2. Investing Activities: Cash related to purchase and sale of fixed assets and investments, such as machinery, land, buildings and securities.
  3. Financing Activities: Cash related to raising and repayment of finance, such as issue of shares, debentures, borrowing and repayment of loans, and payment of dividends.

Thus, a cash flow statement helps management and investors evaluate the firm's liquidity, solvency and ability to generate cash.

(b) Stock Split and Stock Dividend

Stock Split

A stock split means dividing the existing shares of a company into a larger number of shares by reducing their face value.

Example: One share of Rs. 100 may be split into two shares of Rs. 50 each.

  • Number of shares increases.
  • Face value per share decreases.
  • Total share capital remains unchanged.
  • It makes shares more affordable and may increase marketability.

Stock Dividend

A stock dividend, also called a bonus share, means distribution of additional shares to existing shareholders instead of paying cash dividend.

  • Shareholders receive additional shares free of cost.
  • No cash leaves the company.
  • Reserves are converted into share capital.
  • It increases the number of outstanding shares.

Difference: In a stock split, only the face value and number of shares change, whereas in a stock dividend, additional shares are issued out of reserves.

(c) Business Risk and Financial Risk

Business Risk

Business risk is the risk of a firm's operating income being uncertain because of factors related to its normal business operations.

Causes include:

  • Changes in demand and sales
  • Changes in selling price
  • Increase in operating costs
  • Competition
  • High fixed operating costs

Business risk exists even when a firm has no debt.

Financial Risk

Financial risk is the additional risk faced by shareholders because the company uses debt or other fixed-interest-bearing funds.

Causes include:

  • High amount of borrowing
  • Fixed interest obligations
  • Repayment of debt
  • Preference dividend obligations

Higher debt results in higher financial risk because interest must be paid even when profits are low.

Difference: Business risk arises from business operations, while financial risk arises from the financing or capital structure of the firm.

Exam tip: Choose any two. Stock Split & Stock Dividend and Business Risk & Financial Risk are especially easy to present with clear definitions and differences.

Question: Explain capital structure. Mention factors to be considered when making capital structure decision.

Answer

Meaning of Capital Structure

Capital structure refers to the proportion or mix of different long-term sources of finance used by a company, such as equity share capital, preference share capital, debentures and long-term debt.

The main objective of capital structure decision is to select an appropriate mix of debt and equity so as to minimize the cost of capital and maximize the value of the firm.

Factors to be Considered while Making Capital Structure Decision

  1. Cost of Capital: The company should choose sources of finance having a lower overall cost. Debt is generally cheaper than equity because interest is usually tax-deductible.
  2. Financial Risk: Excessive debt increases financial risk because interest and principal must be paid regularly.
  3. Cash Flow Position: A company with stable and sufficient cash flows can use more debt because it can meet regular interest and repayment obligations.
  4. Control of the Company: Issuing new equity shares may dilute the control of existing shareholders, while debt financing normally does not affect ownership control.
  5. Flexibility: The capital structure should provide flexibility so that the company can raise additional funds in the future.
  6. Tax Consideration: Interest paid on debt is generally allowed as an expense for tax purposes, providing a tax advantage.
  7. Nature and Stability of Business: Companies with stable sales and earnings can use more debt, whereas firms with uncertain income should depend more on equity.
  8. Market Conditions: When the share market is favorable, equity may be issued; when interest rates are low, debt may be preferred.
  9. Growth and Expansion Plans: Fast-growing firms require more funds and should maintain a capital structure that allows them to raise finance easily.
  10. Asset Structure: Firms having more fixed assets can often borrow more because such assets can be offered as security.

Conclusion: A company should select a balanced combination of debt and equity by considering cost, risk, control, cash flow, tax benefits and market conditions. An optimum capital structure helps increase profitability and maximize shareholders' wealth.

Question: Explain why Business Entity Concept and Periodicity Concept are important.

Answer

1. Business Entity Concept

The Business Entity Concept states that a business and its owner are treated as separate entities for accounting purposes. Therefore, only business transactions are recorded in the books of accounts.

Importance:

  • It separates business transactions from personal transactions of the owner.
  • Capital introduced by the owner is treated as a liability of the business to the owner.
  • Personal expenses of the owner are treated as drawings, not business expenses.
  • It helps in finding the true profit or loss of the business.
  • It shows the correct financial position of the business.
  • It makes accounting records clear and reliable.

Example: If the owner withdraws Rs. 20,000 for personal use, it is recorded as drawings and not as a business expense.

2. Periodicity Concept

The Periodicity Concept states that the life of a business is divided into specific accounting periods, usually one year, for measuring financial performance.

Importance:

  • It helps determine profit or loss periodically.
  • Financial statements can be prepared at regular intervals.
  • It enables comparison of performance between different years.
  • It helps management in planning and decision-making.
  • It allows investors, creditors and other users to obtain timely financial information.
  • It helps in calculating and paying taxes for a particular accounting period.

Example: A company may prepare its financial statements for the year ending 31st March every year.

Conclusion: The Business Entity Concept ensures separation between the owner and the business, while the Periodicity Concept ensures that business performance is measured at regular intervals. Both are essential for accurate and meaningful accounting information.

Question 3: Describe the concept of dividend and various policies of dividend payment. Also point out and describe the factors affecting dividend policy of a firm.

12 Marks
Answer

1. Meaning / Concept of Dividend

A dividend is the portion of a company's profit that is distributed among its shareholders as a return on their investment. After earning profit, a company may either distribute it as dividend or retain it in the business for future growth and expansion.

The decision regarding how much profit should be distributed and how much should be retained is called the dividend policy of the firm.

The main objective of dividend policy is to maintain a proper balance between:

  • Current income to shareholders, and
  • Future growth of the company through retained earnings.

2. Various Policies of Dividend Payment

(i) Stable Dividend Policy

Under this policy, the company pays a fixed or steadily increasing dividend per share every year, even if profits fluctuate.

  • Provides regular income to shareholders.
  • Creates confidence among investors.
  • Helps maintain stability in the market price of shares.

(ii) Constant Dividend Payout Ratio Policy

Under this policy, the company pays a fixed percentage of its earnings as dividend.

Example: If the payout ratio is 40%, the company distributes 40% of its profit as dividend every year.

  • Dividend increases when profit increases.
  • Dividend decreases when profit falls.
  • Shareholders' income may fluctuate from year to year.

(iii) Regular Dividend Plus Extra Dividend Policy

Under this policy, a company pays a minimum regular dividend every year and an additional or extra dividend in years of high profit. It provides shareholders with regular return while giving the company flexibility during profitable years.

(iv) Residual Dividend Policy

Under this policy, the company first uses its earnings to finance profitable investment opportunities. The remaining profit, if any, is distributed as dividend.

Dividend = Earnings − Amount Required for Investment

This policy gives priority to the growth and investment needs of the firm.

3. Factors Affecting Dividend Policy of a Firm

  1. Earnings of the Company: A firm earning high and stable profits can generally pay higher dividends.
  2. Stability of Earnings: Companies with stable earnings can follow a stable dividend policy, while firms with fluctuating earnings generally pay conservative dividends.
  3. Cash Flow Position: Dividend requires cash payment. Even a profitable company may not pay a high dividend if it does not have sufficient cash.
  4. Growth and Investment Opportunities: A growing company may retain a larger portion of its earnings to finance expansion, new projects and modernization.
  5. Shareholders' Preference: Some shareholders prefer regular dividends, while others prefer capital appreciation.
  6. Access to Capital Market: Companies that can easily raise external funds may pay higher dividends, while firms with limited access may retain more earnings.
  7. Legal Restrictions: Dividend payment must comply with company law and other legal regulations.
  8. Contractual Restrictions: Loan agreements may impose restrictions on dividend payments to protect lenders and creditors.
  9. Tax Considerations: The tax treatment of dividends and capital gains may influence the dividend policy adopted by the company.
  10. Control of the Company: Retaining earnings can reduce the need to issue new shares and therefore help existing owners avoid dilution of control.
  11. Inflation: During inflation, more funds may be required to replace assets and maintain operations, so firms may retain more earnings.
  12. General Economic and Market Conditions: During recession or uncertain conditions, firms may preserve cash and follow a conservative dividend policy.

Conclusion: Dividend policy is an important financial decision because it affects both shareholder satisfaction and future growth. An appropriate policy should provide a reasonable return to shareholders while retaining sufficient profit to meet the company's investment and financial requirements.
