1. Explain why wealth maximization objective is superior to profit maximization objective. 12 marks

The main objective of financial management is to make decisions that increase the value of the firm. Traditionally, firms aimed at profit maximization, but modern financial management considers wealth maximization a better and more appropriate objective.

Profit maximization means earning the highest possible amount of profit for the business. Wealth maximization, on the other hand, means maximizing the market value of shareholders’ investment or increasing the value of the firm.

Reasons why wealth maximization is superior

  1. Considers the time value of money: Wealth maximization recognizes that money received today is more valuable than the same amount received in the future. Profit maximization does not clearly consider the timing of profits.
  2. Considers risk and uncertainty: Every investment involves some degree of risk. Wealth maximization considers both expected return and the risk associated with that return.
  3. Focuses on cash flows: Wealth maximization gives importance to actual cash inflows and outflows rather than only accounting profit.
  4. Takes a long-term perspective: It aims at increasing the value of the firm over the long term, while profit maximization may encourage short-term decisions.
  5. Maximizes shareholders’ wealth: It directly aims to increase the value of shareholders’ investment through higher share prices and returns.
  6. Profit is an ambiguous concept: Profit may mean gross profit, operating profit, profit before tax, profit after tax, or earnings per share. Wealth maximization has a clearer objective.
  7. Considers the quality of benefits: Two projects may generate the same profit, but one may provide returns earlier or involve less risk. Wealth maximization helps select the better project.
  8. Helps in better investment decisions: It uses techniques such as Net Present Value (NPV), which considers future cash flows, time value of money, and risk.
  9. Promotes sustainable growth: It encourages investment in research, technology, employee development, customer satisfaction, and other areas that strengthen long-term performance.
  10. Reflects the overall value of the firm: Market value reflects investors’ expectations regarding profitability, growth, risk, and future prospects.

Conclusion: Wealth maximization is superior to profit maximization because it considers time value of money, risk, cash flows, long-term growth, and shareholders’ interests. It is therefore considered the more comprehensive objective of modern financial management.

8. Describe the types of the dividend policy of a firm. 8 marks

A dividend policy refers to the policy adopted by a firm regarding how much of its earnings should be distributed to shareholders as dividends and how much should be retained in the business.

  1. Stable Dividend Policy: The firm pays a fixed or steadily increasing dividend every year, irrespective of small fluctuations in profit.
  2. Constant Dividend Payout Ratio Policy: The firm pays a fixed percentage of its earnings as dividends. If profit increases, dividend increases; if profit decreases, dividend also decreases.
  3. Regular Dividend plus Extra Dividend Policy: The firm pays a regular minimum dividend every year and an additional dividend when earnings are high.
  4. Residual Dividend Policy: The firm first uses earnings to finance profitable investment opportunities and distributes the remaining earnings as dividends.
  5. Irregular Dividend Policy: The company does not follow a fixed dividend pattern. Payment depends on profit, liquidity, investment opportunities, and other conditions.
  6. No-Dividend Policy: Growth-oriented firms may retain the entire profit for expansion and development instead of distributing dividends.

Conclusion: A firm selects its dividend policy according to profitability, liquidity, growth opportunities, and shareholders’ expectations.

9. Describe the steps of preparation of cash flow statement under direct method. 8 marks

A cash flow statement shows the inflow and outflow of cash and cash equivalents during an accounting period. Under the direct method, major classes of cash receipts and cash payments are shown separately.

  1. Determine cash and cash equivalents: Find the opening balance of cash and cash equivalents.
  2. Calculate cash flow from operating activities: Identify actual cash received and paid in normal business operations.
  3. Calculate cash receipts from customers: Determine cash collected from sales and debtors.
  4. Calculate cash payments to suppliers: Find the amount paid for purchases of goods and services.
  5. Calculate cash payments to employees and for operating expenses: Include wages, salaries, administrative expenses, and other operating payments.
  6. Adjust for tax and interest: Cash paid for income tax and interest is shown according to the applicable accounting standard.
  7. Calculate cash flow from investing activities: Include purchase and sale of fixed assets, purchase and sale of investments, and interest/dividend received where applicable.
  8. Calculate cash flow from financing activities: Include issue and redemption of shares/debentures, borrowings and repayment of loans, and dividend/interest payments where applicable.
  9. Find net increase or decrease in cash: Add net cash flows from operating, investing, and financing activities.
  10. Determine closing cash balance: Opening cash balance + Net change in cash = Closing cash balance.

10. Point out the uses and limitations of ratio analysis. 8 marks

Ratio analysis is a technique of analyzing financial statements by establishing relationships between different accounting figures.

Uses of Ratio Analysis

  1. Measures profitability: Ratios help evaluate the earning capacity of a business.
  2. Measures liquidity: Current ratio and quick ratio show the ability of the firm to meet short-term obligations.
  3. Measures solvency: Ratios help determine the long-term financial stability of the firm.
  4. Helps comparison: Ratios enable comparison between different years and between different firms.
  5. Assists management in decision-making: Management can use ratios for planning, control, and financial decisions.
  6. Evaluates operating efficiency: Turnover ratios indicate how efficiently assets and resources are being utilized.

Limitations of Ratio Analysis

  1. Based on historical data: Ratios are calculated from past financial statements and may not predict future performance.
  2. Different accounting policies: Different depreciation or inventory valuation methods can make comparison difficult.
  3. Ignores qualitative factors: Ratios do not consider factors such as management quality, employee efficiency, and customer satisfaction.
  4. Effect of inflation: Changes in price levels can reduce the usefulness of ratios.
  5. Possibility of window dressing: Financial statements may be manipulated to show favorable ratios.
  6. No standard ratio for all firms: An ideal ratio may differ from one industry to another.

Conclusion: Ratio analysis is a useful tool, but it should be used together with other financial and qualitative information.

11. Write down the rules of double entry-equation rule and types of account rule. 8 marks

A. Double-entry rule

The double-entry system is based on the principle that every transaction has two aspects.

  1. Every transaction affects at least two accounts.
  2. One account is debited and another account is credited.
  3. Total debit must always be equal to total credit.
  4. Assets = Liabilities + Capital.
  5. An increase in assets is debited, while a decrease in assets is credited.
  6. An increase in liabilities and capital is credited, while a decrease is debited.
  7. Expenses and losses are debited.
  8. Income and gains are credited.

Accounting Equation: Assets = Capital + Liabilities

B. Types of accounts and their rules

  1. Personal Account: Accounts relating to persons, firms, companies, and institutions.
    Rule: Debit the receiver, Credit the giver.
  2. Real Account: Accounts relating to assets and properties.
    Rule: Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out.
  3. Nominal Account: Accounts relating to expenses, losses, incomes, and gains.
    Rule: Debit all expenses and losses, Credit all incomes and gains.

12. Describe the various types of costs encountered while managing receivable by a firm. 8 marks

Receivables arise when a firm sells goods or services on credit. Proper management of receivables involves several types of costs.

  1. Collection Cost: Expenses incurred in collecting money from customers, such as salaries of collection staff, postage, telephone expenses, and legal charges.
  2. Capital Cost: Credit sales block funds in receivables. The firm incurs an opportunity cost because these funds could have been invested elsewhere.
  3. Delinquency Cost: This arises when customers do not pay on the due date. The firm incurs additional costs for reminders, follow-up, and collection.
  4. Default or Bad-Debt Cost: Some customers may fail to pay completely, creating irrecoverable bad-debt losses.
  5. Administrative Cost: Costs incurred for maintaining customer accounts, checking creditworthiness, billing, and maintaining credit records.
  6. Credit Investigation Cost: Expenses incurred to obtain credit reports and assess the financial position of customers before granting credit.
  7. Cash Discount Cost: Discounts offered to customers for early payment represent a cost to the firm.
  8. Opportunity Cost: Funds tied up in receivables cannot be used for other profitable purposes, resulting in a loss of potential income.

Conclusion: A firm must balance the benefits of additional credit sales against the various costs associated with receivables.

13. Describe the various factors influencing the working capital requirements of a firm. 8 marks

Working capital refers to the funds required for the day-to-day operations of a business. The amount of working capital required differs from firm to firm.

  1. Nature of business: Trading firms generally require more working capital than service firms because they maintain inventories.
  2. Size of business: Larger firms generally require more working capital due to a higher volume of operations.
  3. Production cycle: A longer production cycle requires more working capital because funds remain blocked for a longer period.
  4. Business cycle: During boom periods, sales and production increase, increasing working capital requirements. During recession, requirements may decline.
  5. Credit policy: Liberal credit terms to customers increase receivables and therefore increase working capital requirements.
  6. Credit available from suppliers: Longer credit periods provided by suppliers reduce the firm's working capital requirement.
  7. Inventory policy: A firm maintaining large inventories requires more working capital.
  8. Seasonal fluctuations: Seasonal businesses require higher working capital during peak seasons.
  9. Growth and expansion: A growing business needs additional working capital to finance increased production and sales.
  10. Operating efficiency: Efficient firms can manage inventories, receivables, and cash better and therefore require less working capital.
  11. Price-level changes: Inflation increases the cost of raw materials, wages, and other expenses, increasing working capital requirements.
  12. Availability of finance: Easy availability of bank credit may reduce the need to maintain a large amount of working capital.

Conclusion: Working capital requirements depend on both internal and external factors. Proper estimation is necessary to maintain liquidity and smooth business operations.
